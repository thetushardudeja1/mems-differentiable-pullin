TITLE
Cutting MEMS Drive Voltage by 41 Percent by Differentiating Through Pull-In


PROBLEM

Most commercial MEMS actuators move by electrostatic force: micromirror
arrays, RF switches, tunable capacitors, optical attenuators. All of them run
into the same wall, which is pull-in.

Below a critical voltage the elastic restoring force of the structure balances
the electrostatic attraction. Above it nothing balances it, and the structure
snaps onto the electrode. Two costs follow.

The first is voltage. Holding a useful deflection without crossing pull-in
takes tens of volts, far above any logic supply, so the die has to carry a
charge pump. That is silicon area, quiescent power, and one more block that
can fail.

The second is travel. Stable motion stops at one third of the gap, and exactly
where that third falls depends on film thickness, residual stress and etch
bias, all of which drift across a wafer. A device's own limit cannot be
measured without pushing it past that limit, which destroys it. So the drive
schedule gets set for the worst die on the wafer, and every good die is driven
below what it could take.

Designers work around this by picking a geometry family by hand and running
FEM sweeps one case at a time, in tools that cost a licence and return a
number but no derivative. Recent AI work in MEMS inherits the same handicap.
The usual recipe is to run on the order of ten thousand FEM simulations, fit a
neural surrogate to them, and then optimise through the surrogate, which means
optimising through its approximation error as well.


THE AI APPROACH

We make the physics differentiable, including the instability.

Pull-in is not the maximum of a smooth curve. It is a saddle-node
bifurcation, a fold, and at the fold the tangent stiffness of the system is
singular. That is precisely where automatic differentiation of a forward solve
falls apart, which is why the obvious approach does not work and why this
problem has stayed on the surrogate side of the field.

Instead of creeping up on the fold we solve for it. The extended fold system
of Keller and Seydel adds two equations to the equilibrium residual: one
saying the Jacobian has a null vector, one fixing that vector's length.

    R(Y, L) = 0,    J(Y, L) v = 0,    ||v|| = 1

Solved together, these turn the bifurcation into an ordinary root of a regular
system. Newton converges on it directly, and the implicit function theorem
then hands back the exact derivative of pull-in voltage with respect to every
point of the electrode gap. One reverse pass, sixty nodes, no finite
differences and no fitted model in between.

Because that gradient is exact and costs almost nothing, three capabilities
follow, and none of them needs a training set.

Inverse design descends on the gap profile against the real pull-in voltage.

An amortized design network maps a specification straight to a geometry. It is
trained by backpropagating through the solver, so every gradient it ever sees
comes from a live solve. Nothing is stored.

A reinforcement learning policy watches how a device responds and infers the
fabrication limit that cannot be measured, then drives closer to it.


NOVELTY

Nobody differentiates through the MEMS pull-in bifurcation. Differentiable
solvers exist in adjacent areas. JAX-FEM, JAX-MPM and JaxSSO all provide
gradients through FEM, meshfree and structural problems. None of them targets
electrostatic pull-in, and none differentiates a fold point.

The design network is trained against physics rather than against an archive.
This inverts the standard pipeline. It also exposed a subtlety worth
reporting: a soft penalty on the travel specification is biased here, because
shrinking travel also shrinks pull-in voltage, so the optimiser is rewarded
for missing the spec. It settles around five percent error. Replacing the
penalty with a differentiable constraint layer, in the style of OptNet and
HardNet, drops that to 0.56 percent.

The RL problem is unusual in a way the literature has not covered. Sequential
experimental design assumes you can measure and continue. Safe exploration
assumes a constraint you can observe. Here the measurement is the failure. An
agent learns the most from the device it just destroyed.


IMPACT ON MEMS

Drive voltage falls from 22.86 V to 13.60 V, a 41 percent reduction, on the
same beam meeting the same 2 um travel specification. That comes entirely from
reshaping the electrode gap. No new material, no extra process step, no extra
mask. In fabrication terms it is free.

Starting from a uniform gap, knowing nothing about any published design
family, the optimiser walks down to the hand-tuned profile that Haluzan and
colleagues arrived at in 2010, and it takes fifteen seconds on a laptop CPU
with no licence of any kind. Their paper closed with a conjecture they could
not test, that gains beyond simple shapes "are believed to be minimal". We
measured what is left: 0.14 percent for the cantilever and 1.45 percent for
the fixed-fixed beam. They were right, and now it is a number.

On the operating side, the policy recovers 80.4 percent of the travel that
conservative fixed-gain control gives away, which is 12.8 percent more usable
stroke, across 1536 held-out test episodes with no device destroyed.

Once trained, the network emits a geometry in 0.04 ms and meets the
specification to 0.56 percent, so a design that was a meeting and a simulation
queue becomes a function call.


EVIDENCE

The solver is checked against eight independent sources published between 1967
and 2025. The closed-form pull-in threshold comes out to 0.001 percent. The
exact gradient reproduces an analytic cube law to 0.00 percent, which is the
check that matters most, because it tests the differentiation rather than the
forward solve. Three of three instability mechanisms from Seeger and Boser are
predicted correctly, including a pair of designs that are mechanically
identical and differ only in parasitic capacitance, where the model correctly
calls a different failure mode for each.

The sharpest test is a reversal. The optimal gap exponent moves the opposite
way for the two boundary conditions: roughly 4/3 for a cantilever, exactly 1
for a fixed-fixed beam. We reproduce both. Opposite trends in two geometries
are not something a fitted model produces by accident.

Three things are worth stating plainly. Our absolute voltages sit high by a
systematic margin, which we traced to anchor compliance that a one-dimensional
ideal-clamp model cannot represent. A well-built Fourier neural operator
surrogate is competitive with us on design quality at equal budget. And the
static solver needs float64, so it runs on CPU rather than GPU. All three are
quantified in the report.

Everything is released: solver, validation suite, RL environment, the
surrogate baselines we built to compete against ourselves, a ninety-second
notebook, and the figure pipeline.
