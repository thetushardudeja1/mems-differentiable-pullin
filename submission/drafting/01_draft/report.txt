TITLE
Cutting MEMS Drive Voltage by 41 Percent: Inverse Design Through the Pull-In
Bifurcation


ABSTRACT

Electrostatic MEMS actuators are limited by pull-in, a snap-through
instability that caps stable travel at a third of the gap and destroys the
device when crossed. Designing around it is expensive because pull-in is a
saddle-node bifurcation, where the tangent stiffness is singular and a
differentiable forward solve breaks down, so the field has settled on fitting
neural surrogates to large FEM campaigns and optimising through them. We solve
the extended fold system directly, which makes the bifurcation a root of a
regular system and gives exact derivatives of pull-in voltage with respect to
electrode geometry through the implicit function theorem. Three capabilities
follow, none of which uses a training set. Inverse design cuts drive voltage
from 22.86 V to 13.60 V, a 41 percent reduction, on the same beam meeting the
same travel specification and with no change to the process. An amortized
network trained end to end through the solver emits a geometry in 0.04 ms and
meets the specification to 0.56 percent. A reinforcement learning policy
infers each device's unmeasurable fabrication limit and recovers 12.8 percent
more usable travel with no device destroyed. The solver is validated against
eight independent sources spanning 1967 to 2025, and reproduces a reversal in
the optimal gap exponent between cantilever and fixed-fixed geometries.


1. PROBLEM

Electrostatic actuation dominates commercial MEMS, and pull-in bounds what it
can do. Below a critical voltage the elastic restoring force balances the
electrostatic attraction; above it nothing does, and the structure collapses
onto the electrode.

Two costs follow. Drive voltages run to tens of volts, above any logic supply,
so the die carries a charge pump that consumes area and power and adds a
failure mode. And stable travel ends at a third of the gap, at a threshold
that shifts with film thickness, residual stress and etch bias. That threshold
cannot be measured on a device without destroying it, so drive schedules are
set for the worst die and the rest of the wafer is under-driven.

Design practice meets this with FEM sweeps over hand-picked geometry families,
in tools that return a number and no derivative. Recent AI work inherits the
handicap: fit a surrogate to roughly ten thousand simulations, then optimise
through the surrogate and its approximation error. Figure 1 shows what we do
instead.


2. METHODOLOGY

2.1 Governing equations

A beam of plate modulus E and thickness t over a shaped electrode gap d(x)
obeys, after nondimensionalisation with x = l xi, y = d0 Y and G = D - Y,

    Y'''' - Nt Y'' = Lambda (1 + alpha G) / G^2

where Lambda = 6 eps0 V^2 l^4 / (E t^3 d0^3) is the normalised electrostatic
load, Nt carries residual-stress tension and alpha is a fringing-field
correction. Fourth-order finite differences reduce this to a residual
R(Y, Lambda) on the interior nodes.

2.2 Differentiating through the fold

Pull-in is a turning point of the equilibrium branch rather than a maximum of
a smooth function. At that point the tangent stiffness J = dR/dY is singular,
which is exactly where a differentiated forward solve fails: the linear solve
inside it is the thing that blows up. This is the reason the problem has
stayed on the surrogate side of the literature.

We solve for the fold instead of approaching it. Following Keller and Seydel,
the equilibrium residual is extended with a null-vector condition and a
normalisation,

    R(Y, Lambda) = 0,    J(Y, Lambda) v = 0,    v^T v - 1 = 0

and the three are solved together for (Y, Lambda, v) in one Newton solve, warm
started from a coarse displacement-controlled sweep. The bifurcation is now an
ordinary root of a regular system, so the implicit function theorem applies
and returns dLambda/dd(x) exactly. It is not a finite difference and not a
surrogate estimate.

Globalisation is what makes this usable rather than merely correct. The solver
uses an Armijo line search with a fraction-to-the-boundary rule, and row
equilibration in the displacement-controlled stage. Without equilibration an
O(1) constraint row sits alongside beam rows of order 1e6 and Newton stalls on
shaped profiles. One detail earns its place: the gap floor is 1e-12 rather
than 1e-6, so a state in which the beam has passed through the electrode
produces an enormous residual and is rejected, instead of converging quietly
onto a nonphysical branch.


3. DATASETS

There is no training dataset, and that is the methodological claim rather than
an omission. Every gradient that trains every network in this work comes from
a live solve of the fold system. The amortized network of Section 4 consumed
6000 live solves and stored none of them.

Two kinds of data do appear and we keep them separate. The surrogate baselines
in Section 5.3 are the only components that consume sampled data, 400 solves
each, generated on demand under active learning by the scripts we ship.
Validation targets are numbers transcribed from the literature, geometry,
material properties and measured pull-in voltages, each cited where it is used
in the source file. Third-party PDFs are not redistributed; the index in
papers/README.md carries full citations and DOIs.


4. MODEL ARCHITECTURE

4.1 Amortized design network

The gap profile is parameterised as d(xi) = softplus(sum_k p_k cos(k pi xi))
over K = 12 cosine modes on N = 40 nodes, together with a scalar scale sigma.
The network maps a specification, the required travel T, onto that
parameterisation. It takes two inputs, T normalised to its range and log T,
passes them through a 2-64-64 tanh trunk, and splits into two linear heads:
one for the K shape coefficients, one for log sigma.

4.2 The hard-constraint layer

A soft penalty on the travel specification is biased here, and the bias has a
direction. Reducing travel also reduces pull-in voltage, so the optimiser is
paid to miss the specification, and it settles near five percent error.

We replace the penalty with a differentiable enforcement layer, in the spirit
of OptNet and HardNet. Four Newton steps solve travel(p, sigma) = T for sigma,
and a fifth correction step is taken with the p-dependence live while
d(travel)/d(sigma) is held fixed. At convergence that step reproduces
dsigma*/dp = -F_p / F_sigma, the same implicit-function argument used for the
fold system itself. The constraint is satisfied exactly and the shape head
still receives a correct gradient. The loss contains no penalty term at all.
The scale head is regressed onto a stop-gradient copy of the layer's answer so
that inference stays a single forward pass. Specification error falls from
about five percent to 0.56 percent across T = 1 to 3 um.

4.3 Baselines and control policy

The strongest baseline is a one-dimensional Fourier neural operator, width 32,
12 retained modes, depth 4. We gave it active learning, a trust region and
feasible restarts, because our first comparison was a strawman: without a
trust region the surrogate proposed designs where the true solver returned a
travel of -639458, and beating that would have proved nothing. An MLP
surrogate is included for reference.

The control policy is a 7-64-1 tanh network trained by REINFORCE with a
batch-mean baseline and Gaussian exploration of standard deviation 0.10.
Pathwise gradients do not apply because episode termination is hard and
irreversible.


5. RESULTS

5.1 Validation

Table I and Figure 4 summarise agreement with eight independent sources
published between 1967 and 2025. Two entries carry most of the weight.

The exact gradient reproduces the analytic cube scaling law to 0.00 percent.
This is the check that tests differentiation rather than the forward solve,
and it is the one that would have caught a plausible-looking but wrong
adjoint.

The optimal gap exponent reverses between geometries, as Figure 2(b) and 2(c)
show: 1.29 for the cantilever against a reported 4/3, and 1.00 for the
fixed-fixed beam, matching the monotone trend reported for that case. A model
tuned to reproduce one trend does not produce the opposite trend in the other
geometry by accident.

Seeger and Boser supply a discriminating case. Two of their designs are
mechanically identical and differ only in parasitic capacitance. The model
predicts, correctly, that this purely electrical difference changes which
instability arrives first and costs twenty percent of travel.

5.2 Inverse design

Minimising pull-in voltage at fixed stable travel, by gradient descent on a
free-form profile with the scale set by the constraint layer so that every
iterate is feasible, takes the cantilever benchmark from 22.86 V to 13.60 V.
That is 41 percent, from electrode geometry alone, with no new material,
process step or mask.

Starting from a uniform gap and given nothing about any prior design family,
the optimiser rediscovers the hand-tuned profile of Haluzan et al. (Figure
2(a)) and descends from 22.9 V to 13.9 V unaided, which rules out a
favourable initialisation doing the work. Table II reports both boundary
conditions. Two quantities were matched without being fitted: a maximum gap of
6.571 um against their 6.57, and a flattened-bottom ratio of 0.80 against
their 0.78.

The same machinery settles a question their paper left open. They conjectured
that gains beyond simple shapes "are believed to be minimal" but had no way to
test it. Free-form optimisation over the whole profile measures what remains:
0.14 percent for the cantilever, 1.45 percent for the fixed-fixed beam.

5.3 Comparison with surrogate modelling

At an equal budget of 400 true solves, an MLP surrogate reaches 17.20 V and
the FNO reaches 14.25 V, against 13.78 V for direct differentiation and
13.64 V for the amortized network (Table III). The FNO is competitive, and we
say so. Our advantage is that we need no training data, no architecture
search, and 40 times less wall-clock, not that we produce a better design.

5.4 Recovering travel lost to tolerance

The tip-in ceiling r = 1 / (1 + kx Lc^2 / 6 ktheta) varies from device to
device and cannot be observed at test time, so fixed-gain control has to aim
at the worst case and forfeits 19 percent of travel. A policy that sees only
the device's own tilt response recovers 80.4 plus or minus 4.9 percent of that
range, which is 12.8 percent more usable travel, over 1536 held-out episodes
with 0.0 plus or minus 0.0 percent of devices destroyed.

Reduced conservatism alone would produce the same average. What shows genuine
adaptation is the correlation between the travel a device achieves and that
same device's own ceiling: 0.96 for the policy against -0.03 for fixed gain
(Figure 3(a)). The policy is tracking something it cannot measure.


6. LIMITATIONS

Absolute voltages carry a systematic offset. M-TEST comes out 5.4 percent
high, and the 2025 microbridge about 85 percent high, where matching would
require an effective length of 162 um against a drawn 120 um. The evidence
points at anchor compliance, which an ideal-clamp one-dimensional model cannot
represent. Relative and trend predictions agree to within 2.8 percentage
points, which is why the 41 percent above is stated as a relative claim on one
process rather than an absolute prediction.

The static solver needs float64. In fp32 it does not converge, with a residual
of 2.0 against 1e-8, and consumer GPUs run fp64 at roughly a sixty-fourth of
their fp32 rate, so this workload is CPU-bound by nature. The GPU earns its
place only on the fp32 dynamics and RL training.

The amortized network matches converged optimisation rather than beating it,
13.636 V against 13.665 V, which sits inside the 0.28 percent spread across
optimiser restarts.

Fixed-fixed profiles with exponent above roughly 1.8 are infeasible rather
than merely expensive: travel saturates below the specification at any scale.
We widened the search bracket from 30 to 80 to confirm this before excluding
them, rather than plotting a curve that would have implied they were simply
costly.
