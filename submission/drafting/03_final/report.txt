TITLE
Cutting MEMS Drive Voltage by 41 Percent: Inverse Design Through the Pull-In
Bifurcation


ABSTRACT

Pull-in caps the stable travel of an electrostatic MEMS actuator at a third of
its gap and destroys the device when crossed. It is also a saddle-node
bifurcation, where the tangent stiffness is singular and a differentiated
forward solve breaks down, which is why the field designs around it with
neural surrogates fitted to large FEM campaigns. We solve the extended fold
system directly. That makes the bifurcation an ordinary root of a regular
system, and the implicit function theorem then gives exact derivatives of
pull-in voltage with respect to electrode geometry. Three capabilities follow,
none of them using a training set. Inverse design cuts drive voltage from
22.86 V to 13.60 V, a 41 percent reduction on the same beam meeting the same
travel specification, with no change to the process. An amortized network
trained end to end through the solver emits a geometry in 0.04 ms and holds
the specification to 0.56 percent. A control policy infers each device's
unmeasurable fabrication limit and recovers 12.8 percent more usable travel
with no device destroyed. We validate the solver against eight independent
sources spanning 1967 to 2025, including a reproduced reversal of the optimal
gap exponent between cantilever and fixed-fixed geometries.


1. PROBLEM

Electrostatic actuation dominates commercial MEMS, and pull-in bounds what it
can deliver. Past a critical voltage the electrostatic attraction outruns the
elastic restoring force and the structure collapses onto the electrode.

Two costs follow. The first is drive voltage: staying safely below pull-in at
a useful deflection takes tens of volts, so the die carries a charge pump that
spends area and power and adds a failure mode. The second is travel, which
ends at a third of the gap, at a threshold that shifts with film thickness,
residual stress and etch bias. Measuring that threshold on a given device
requires crossing it, which destroys the device, so drive schedules are set for
the worst die and the rest of the wafer is under-driven.

Design practice meets this with FEM sweeps over hand-picked geometry families,
in tools that return a number and no derivative. Recent AI work inherits the
handicap: fit a surrogate to roughly ten thousand simulations, then optimise
through the surrogate and its approximation error. Fig. 1 shows what we do
instead.


2. METHODOLOGY

2.1 Governing equations

A beam of plate modulus E and thickness t over a shaped electrode gap d(x)
obeys, after nondimensionalisation with x = l xi, y = d0 Y and G = D - Y,

    Y'''' - Nt Y'' = Lambda (1 + alpha G) / G^2

where Lambda = 6 eps0 V^2 l^4 / (E t^3 d0^3) is the normalised electrostatic
load, Nt carries residual-stress tension, and alpha is a fringing-field
correction. Fourth-order finite differences reduce this to a residual
R(Y, Lambda) on the interior nodes.

2.2 Differentiating through the fold

Pull-in is not the maximum of a smooth function. It is a turning point of the
equilibrium branch, and there the tangent stiffness J = dR/dY is singular. A
differentiated forward solve fails at precisely that point, because the linear
solve inside it is the object going singular. This is the reason the problem
has stayed on the surrogate side of the literature.

So we solve for the fold instead of approaching it. Following Keller and
Seydel, the equilibrium residual is extended with a null-vector condition and a
normalisation,

    R(Y, Lambda) = 0,    J(Y, Lambda) v = 0,    v^T v - 1 = 0

and the three are solved together for (Y, Lambda, v) in a single Newton solve,
warm started from a coarse displacement-controlled sweep. The bifurcation is
now an ordinary root of a regular system, so the implicit function theorem
applies and returns dLambda/dd(x) exactly, with no finite differencing and no
fitted model in between.

Correctness is not sufficient on its own here; the solver also has to converge
on profiles it has never seen. It uses an Armijo line search with a
fraction-to-the-boundary rule, and row equilibration in the
displacement-controlled stage. Without equilibration an O(1) constraint row
sits alongside beam rows of order 1e6 and Newton stalls on shaped profiles. The
gap floor is set to 1e-12 and not 1e-6, so a state in which the beam has passed
through the electrode returns an enormous residual and gets rejected, instead
of converging quietly onto a nonphysical branch. Both choices were made after
a failure, not in advance.


3. DATASETS

The exact gradient of Section 2.2 is what removes the need for a dataset, so
this section is short by construction.

There is no training data. Every gradient that trains every network in this
work comes from a live solve of the fold system; the amortized network of
Section 4 consumed 6000 live solves and stored none of them.

Two kinds of data do appear, and we keep them apart. The surrogate baselines in
Section 5.3 are the only components that consume sampled data, 400 solves
each, generated on demand under active learning by the scripts we ship.
Validation targets are numbers transcribed from the literature: geometry,
material properties and measured pull-in voltages, each cited where it is used
in the source file. We do not redistribute third-party PDFs; the index in
papers/README.md carries full citations and DOIs.


4. MODEL ARCHITECTURE

4.1 Amortized design network

The gap profile is parameterised as d(xi) = softplus(sum_k p_k cos(k pi xi))
over K = 12 cosine modes on N = 40 nodes, together with a scalar scale sigma.
The network maps a specification, the required travel T, onto that
parameterisation. Two inputs, T normalised to its range and log T, pass through
a 2-64-64 tanh trunk and split into two linear heads: one for the K shape
coefficients, one for log sigma.

4.2 The hard-constraint layer

A soft penalty on the travel specification is biased here, and the bias has a
direction. Reducing travel also reduces pull-in voltage, so the optimiser gets
paid for missing the specification, and it settles near five percent error. No
amount of weight tuning fixes a gradient that points the wrong way.

We replace the penalty with a differentiable enforcement layer, in the spirit
of OptNet and HardNet. Four Newton steps solve travel(p, sigma) = T for sigma.
A fifth correction step is then taken with the p-dependence live while
d(travel)/d(sigma) is held fixed. At convergence that step reproduces
dsigma*/dp = -F_p / F_sigma, which is the same implicit-function argument used
for the fold system itself. The constraint is satisfied exactly and the shape
head still receives a correct gradient. The loss carries no penalty term at
all. The scale head is regressed onto a stop-gradient copy of the layer's
answer, so inference stays a single forward pass. Specification error falls
from about five percent to 0.56 percent across T = 1 to 3 um.

4.3 Baselines and control policy

The strongest baseline is a one-dimensional Fourier neural operator: width 32,
12 retained modes, depth 4. We gave it active learning, a trust region and
feasible restarts, because our first comparison was a strawman. Without a
trust region the surrogate proposed designs where the true solver returned a
travel of -639458, and beating that would have proved nothing. An MLP surrogate
is included for reference.

The control policy is a 7-64-1 tanh network trained by REINFORCE with a
batch-mean baseline and Gaussian exploration of standard deviation 0.10.
Pathwise gradients do not apply, because episode termination is hard and
irreversible.


5. RESULTS

5.1 Validation

Table I and Fig. 4 summarise agreement with eight independent sources
published between 1967 and 2025.

The exact gradient reproduces the analytic cube scaling law to 0.00 percent.
That check tests the differentiation instead of the forward solve, and it is
the one that would have caught a plausible-looking but wrong adjoint.

The optimal gap exponent reverses between geometries, as Fig. 2(b) and 2(c)
show: 1.29 for the cantilever against a reported 4/3, and 1.00 for the
fixed-fixed beam, matching the monotone trend reported for that case. A model
tuned to reproduce one trend does not produce the opposite trend in the other
geometry by accident.

Seeger and Boser supply a discriminating case. Two of their designs are
mechanically identical and differ only in parasitic capacitance. The model
predicts, correctly, that this purely electrical difference changes which
instability arrives first, and costs twenty percent of travel.

5.2 Inverse design

Minimising pull-in voltage at fixed stable travel, by gradient descent on a
free-form profile with the scale set by the constraint layer so every iterate
stays feasible, takes the cantilever benchmark from 22.86 V to 13.60 V. That
is 41 percent from electrode geometry alone, with no new material, process
step or mask.

Starting from a uniform gap and given nothing about any prior design family,
the optimiser rediscovers the hand-tuned profile of Haluzan et al. (Fig. 2(a))
and descends from 22.9 V to 13.9 V unaided, which rules out a favourable
initialisation doing the work. Table II reports both boundary conditions. Two
quantities came out matched without ever being fitted: a maximum gap of
6.571 um against their 6.57, and a flattened-bottom ratio of 0.80 against
their 0.78.

The same machinery settles a question their paper left open. They conjectured
that gains beyond simple shapes "are believed to be minimal" but had no way to
test it. Free-form optimisation over the whole profile measures what remains:
0.14 percent on the cantilever, 1.45 percent on the fixed-fixed beam.

5.3 Comparison with surrogate modelling

At an equal budget of 400 true solves, an MLP surrogate reaches 17.20 V and
the FNO reaches 14.25 V, against 13.78 V for direct differentiation and
13.64 V for the amortized network (Table III). The FNO is competitive. Our
advantage is that we need no training data, no architecture search and 40
times less wall-clock, not that we produce a better design.

5.4 Recovering travel lost to tolerance

The tip-in ceiling r = 1 / (1 + kx Lc^2 / 6 ktheta) varies from device to
device and cannot be observed at test time, so fixed-gain control has to aim at
the worst case and forfeits 19 percent of travel. A policy that sees only the
device's own tilt response recovers 80.4 +/- 4.9 percent of that range, which
is 12.8 percent more usable travel, over 1536 held-out episodes with
0.0 +/- 0.0 percent of devices destroyed.

Reduced conservatism alone would produce the same average, so the average is
not the evidence. What shows genuine adaptation is the correlation between the
travel a device achieves and that device's own ceiling: 0.96 for the policy
against -0.03 for fixed gain (Fig. 3(a)). The policy is tracking something it
cannot measure.


6. LIMITATIONS

Absolute voltages carry a systematic offset. M-TEST comes out 5.4 percent
high, and the 2025 microbridge about 85 percent high, where matching would
demand an effective length of 162 um against a drawn 120 um. The evidence
points at anchor compliance, which a one-dimensional ideal-clamp model cannot
represent. Relative and trend predictions agree to within 2.8 percentage
points, and that is why the 41 percent above is a relative claim on one
process and not an absolute prediction.

The static solver needs float64. In fp32 it does not converge, leaving a
residual of 2.0 against 1e-8, and consumer GPUs run fp64 at roughly a
sixty-fourth of their fp32 rate, so this workload is CPU-bound by nature. The
GPU earns its place only on the fp32 dynamics and RL training.

The amortized network matches converged optimisation, 13.636 V against
13.665 V, and does not beat it. That difference sits inside the 0.28 percent
spread we see across optimiser restarts.

Fixed-fixed profiles with exponent above roughly 1.8 are infeasible and not
merely expensive: travel saturates below the specification at any scale. We
widened the search bracket from 30 to 80 to confirm it before excluding them,
because plotting them would have implied they were simply costly.
