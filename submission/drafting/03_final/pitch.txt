PROBLEM

Most commercial MEMS actuators move by electrostatic force: micromirror
arrays, RF switches, tunable capacitors, optical attenuators. All of them are
bounded by pull-in.

Below a critical voltage the structure's elastic restoring force balances the
electrostatic attraction. Above it nothing does, and the plate snaps onto the
electrode. Two limits follow. Holding a useful deflection safely takes tens of
volts, well above any logic supply, so the die carries a charge pump that costs
area and quiescent power. And stable motion stops at a third of the gap, at a
threshold that moves with film thickness, residual stress and etch bias. You
cannot find where a device's threshold sits without crossing it, which destroys
the device, so the drive schedule is set for the worst die on the wafer and
every good die is driven below what it could take.

Designers meet this with hand-picked geometry families and FEM sweeps, in tools
that cost a licence and return a number with no derivative attached. Recent AI
work in MEMS inherits the same handicap: fit a neural surrogate to on the order
of ten thousand simulations, then optimise through its approximation error.


THE AI APPROACH

We make the physics differentiable, and that includes the instability.

Pull-in is a saddle-node bifurcation. At the fold the tangent stiffness is
singular, and a differentiated forward solve dies there because the linear
solve inside it is the thing going singular. That is why the obvious approach
fails, and a large part of why this problem has stayed on the surrogate side of
the field.

So we solve for the fold instead of creeping up on it. The extended fold system
of Keller and Seydel adds a null-vector condition and a normalisation to the
equilibrium residual:

    R(Y, L) = 0,    J(Y, L) v = 0,    ||v|| = 1

Together these turn the bifurcation into an ordinary root of a regular system.
Newton lands on it directly, and the implicit function theorem returns the
exact derivative of pull-in voltage with respect to every point of the
electrode gap, in a single reverse pass.

An exact gradient that costs almost nothing changes what is worth attempting.
Inverse design descends on the gap profile against real pull-in voltage instead
of a proxy. An amortized network learns the map from specification to geometry
by backpropagating through the solver, so every gradient it sees comes from a
live solve and nothing is stored. And a reinforcement learning policy watches
how an individual device responds, infers the fabrication limit nobody can
measure, and drives closer to it. None of the three needs a training set.


NOVELTY

Differentiable solvers are not new. JAX-FEM, JAX-MPM and JaxSSO all return
gradients through finite element, meshfree and structural problems, and they
work well. None targets electrostatic pull-in, and none differentiates a fold
point, because the fold is where their machinery stops.

Training a design network against physics instead of an archive also surfaced
something we did not expect. A soft penalty on the travel specification is
biased, and the bias has a direction: shrinking travel shrinks pull-in voltage
as well, so the optimiser gets paid for missing the spec and settles near five
percent error. A differentiable constraint layer, in the style of OptNet and
HardNet, brings that down to 0.56 percent.

The control problem is unusual in a way the literature has not covered.
Sequential experimental design assumes you can measure and carry on. Safe
exploration assumes a constraint you can see. Here the measurement is the
failure. An agent learns the most from the device it just destroyed.


IMPACT ON MEMS

[table: 22.86 -> 13.60 V drive voltage, same mask count; +12.8% usable travel;
0.0% devices destroyed; 15 s design turnaround on a laptop CPU; 0.04 ms per
design once trained]

The 41 percent comes entirely from reshaping the electrode gap: no new
material, no extra process step, no extra mask. In fabrication terms the saving
is free.

Started from a uniform gap and told nothing about any published design family,
the optimiser walks down to the hand-tuned profile that Haluzan and colleagues
reached in 2010, in fifteen seconds on a laptop. Their paper closed with a
conjecture they had no way to test, that gains beyond simple shapes "are
believed to be minimal". We measured what is actually left: 0.14 percent for
the cantilever and 1.45 percent for the fixed-fixed beam. They were right, and
now it is a number.


EVIDENCE

The solver is checked against eight independent sources published between 1967
and 2025, and two of those checks carry the argument. The exact gradient
reproduces an analytic cube law to 0.00 percent, which tests the
differentiation itself rather than the forward solve, so it is what would have
caught a plausible-looking but wrong adjoint. And the optimal gap exponent
reverses between geometries, roughly 4/3 for a cantilever against exactly 1 for
a fixed-fixed beam. We reproduce both. A model tuned to match one trend does
not produce the opposite trend in the other geometry by accident.

Three things we should say out loud. Our absolute voltages sit high by a
systematic margin, traced to anchor compliance that a one-dimensional
ideal-clamp model cannot represent. A well-built Fourier neural operator
surrogate is competitive with us on design quality at equal budget. And the
static solver needs float64, so it runs on CPU and not GPU. All three are
quantified in the report.

Everything is open: solver, validation suite, RL environment, the surrogate
baselines we built to compete against ourselves, and a ninety-second notebook.
No commercial licence is needed at any point, which means anyone at this
conference can clone it and reproduce the 41 percent on a laptop the same
afternoon.
