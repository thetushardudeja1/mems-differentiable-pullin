\begin{abstract}
Pull-in caps the stable travel of an electrostatic MEMS actuator at a third of
its gap and destroys the device when crossed. It is a saddle-node bifurcation,
where the tangent stiffness is singular and a differentiated forward solve
breaks down, which is why the field designs around it with surrogates fitted to
large FEM campaigns. We solve the extended fold system directly, making the
bifurcation an ordinary root of a regular system so that the implicit function
theorem gives exact derivatives of pull-in voltage with respect to electrode
geometry. Three capabilities follow, none using a training set: inverse design,
cutting drive voltage $22.86\rightarrow13.60$\,V ($-41\%$) at the same travel
specification with no process change; an amortized network trained through the
solver, emitting a geometry in $0.04$\,ms to within $0.56\%$ of specification;
and a control policy that infers each device's unmeasurable fabrication limit,
recovering $12.8\%$ more usable travel with no device destroyed. We validate
against eight independent sources spanning 1967--2025, reproducing a reversal
of the optimal gap exponent between geometries.
\end{abstract}

\section{Problem}

Electrostatic actuation dominates commercial MEMS, and pull-in bounds what it
can deliver. Past a critical voltage the electrostatic attraction outruns the
elastic restoring force and the structure collapses onto the electrode.

Two costs follow. Staying safely below pull-in takes tens of volts, so the die
carries a charge pump that spends area and power. And travel ends at a third of
the gap, at a threshold that shifts with film thickness, residual stress and
etch bias. Measuring that threshold requires crossing it, which destroys the
device, so drive schedules are set for the worst die and the rest of the wafer
is under-driven.

Design practice meets this with FEM sweeps over hand-picked geometry families,
in tools that return a number and no derivative. Recent AI work inherits the
handicap: the current standard pipeline \cite{zhang} fits a surrogate to
$48{,}000$ simulations, then optimises through that surrogate and its
approximation error. We remove the intermediary.

\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Governing equations}
A beam of plate modulus $\tilde{E}$ and thickness $t$ over a shaped electrode
gap $d(x)$ obeys, after nondimensionalisation with $x=l\xi$, $y=d_0Y$ and
$G=D-Y$,
\begin{equation}
Y'''' - N_t\,Y'' \;=\; \Lambda\,\frac{1+\alpha G}{G^{2}},
\label{eq:beam}
\end{equation}
where $\Lambda = 6\varepsilon_0V^2l^4/(\tilde{E}t^3d_0^3)$ is the normalised
electrostatic load, $N_t$ carries residual-stress tension, and $\alpha$ is a
fringing-field correction. Fourth-order finite differences reduce this to a
residual $R(Y,\Lambda)$ on the interior nodes.

\subsection{Differentiating through the fold}
Pull-in is not the maximum of a smooth function. It is a turning point of the
equilibrium branch, and there the tangent stiffness $J=\partial R/\partial Y$
is singular. A differentiated forward solve fails at precisely that point,
because the linear solve inside it is the object going singular.

So we solve for the fold instead of approaching it. Following Keller
\cite{keller}, the equilibrium residual is extended with a null-vector
condition and a normalisation,
\begin{equation}
R(Y,\Lambda)=0,\qquad J(Y,\Lambda)\,v=0,\qquad v^{\!\top}v-1=0,
\label{eq:fold}
\end{equation}
and the three are solved together for $(Y,\Lambda,v)$ in a single Newton solve,
warm started from a coarse displacement-controlled sweep. The bifurcation is
now an ordinary root of a regular system, so the implicit function theorem
applies and returns $\partial\Lambda/\partial d(x)$ exactly, with no finite
differencing and no fitted model in between.

Being correct is not the same as converging, and two globalisation choices,
both found by watching a failure, let the solver reach profiles it has never
seen. The first is row equilibration: without it an $O(1)$ constraint row sits
alongside beam rows of order $10^6$ and Newton stalls on shaped profiles. The
second is a gap floor of $10^{-12}$ rather than $10^{-6}$, which makes the
residual of a beam that has passed through the electrode enormous, so that
state is rejected instead of converging onto a nonphysical branch. The stencil
is cancellation-limited, so the solver runs in float64 and is CPU-bound.

\section{Datasets}

The exact gradient of Sec.~II-B removes the need for a dataset, so this section
is short by construction. Every gradient that trains every network here comes
from a live solve of \eqref{eq:fold}; the network of Sec.~IV consumed 6{,}000
live solves and stored none of them. Two kinds of data do appear and we keep
them apart: the surrogate baselines of Sec.~V-C consume 400 sampled solves
each, generated on demand, while validation targets are literature values cited
at the point of use in the source file.

\section{Model architecture}

\subsection{Amortized design network}
The gap profile is parameterised as
$d(\xi)=\mathrm{softplus}\!\left(\sum_{k<K}p_k\cos k\pi\xi\right)$ over $K=12$
cosine modes on $N=40$ nodes, plus a scalar scale $\sigma$. The network maps
the required travel $T$ onto that parameterisation: two inputs, $T$ normalised
to its range and $\log T$, pass through a $2\!\to\!64\!\to\!64$ $\tanh$ trunk
and split into linear heads for the $K$ shape coefficients and for
$\log\sigma$.

\subsection{The hard-constraint layer}
A soft penalty on the travel specification is biased here, and the bias has a
direction. Reducing travel also reduces $V_{\rm PI}$, so the optimiser gets
paid for missing the specification, and it settles near $5\%$ error. No amount
of weight tuning fixes a gradient that points the wrong way.

We replace it with a differentiable enforcement layer, in the spirit of OptNet
\cite{amos} and HardNet. Four Newton steps solve
$\mathrm{travel}(p,\sigma)=T$ for $\sigma$; a fifth correction step is taken
with the $p$-dependence live while $\partial\,\mathrm{travel}/\partial\sigma$
is held fixed. At convergence that reproduces
$d\sigma^\ast/dp=-F_p/F_\sigma$, the same implicit-function argument used for
\eqref{eq:fold} itself, so the constraint holds exactly \emph{and} the shape
head still receives a correct gradient. The loss carries no penalty term, and
the scale head is regressed onto a stop-gradient copy of the layer's answer, so
inference stays one forward pass. Specification error falls from about $5\%$ to
$0.56\%$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:amort}).

\subsection{Baselines and control policy}
The strongest baseline is a 1D Fourier neural operator \cite{li}: width 32, 12
modes, depth 4, given active learning, a trust region and feasible restarts.
Our first comparison was a strawman: without a trust region the surrogate
proposed designs where the true solver returned a travel of $-639458$. The
control policy is a $7\!\to\!64\!\to\!1$ $\tanh$ network trained by REINFORCE
with a batch-mean baseline and Gaussian exploration ($\sigma=0.10$); pathwise
gradients do not apply, because episode termination is irreversible.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Validation}
\input{../figures/tables.tex}

Table~\ref{tab:validation} summarises agreement with eight independent sources
published between 1967 and 2025.

The exact gradient reproduces the analytic $c^3$ scaling law to $0.00\%$. That
check tests the differentiation rather than the forward solve, and is the one
that would have caught a plausible but wrong adjoint.

The optimal gap exponent reverses between geometries
(Fig.~\ref{fig:design}(b),(c)): $n^*\!=\!1.29$ for the cantilever against a
reported $4/3$, and $n^*\!=\!1.00$ for the fixed--fixed beam, matching the
monotone trend reported there. A model tuned to one trend does not produce the
opposite trend in the other geometry by accident.

Seeger and Boser \cite{seeger} supply a discriminating case: two of their
designs are mechanically identical and differ only in parasitic capacitance.
The model correctly predicts that this purely electrical difference changes
which instability arrives first, costing $20\%$ of travel.

\subsection{Inverse design}
Minimising $V_{\rm PI}$ at fixed stable travel, by gradient descent on a
free-form profile with the scale set by the constraint layer so every iterate
stays feasible, takes the cantilever benchmark from $22.86$\,V to $13.60$\,V.
That is $41\%$ from electrode geometry alone, with no new material, process
step or mask.

Starting from a uniform gap and given nothing about any prior design family,
the optimiser rediscovers the hand-tuned profile of \cite{haluzan}
(Fig.~\ref{fig:design}(a)) and descends $22.9\rightarrow13.9$\,V unaided,
ruling out a favourable initialisation. Two quantities came out matched without
ever being fitted: a maximum gap of $6.571\,\mu$m against their $6.57$, and a
flattened-bottom ratio of $0.80$ against $0.78$. Table~\ref{tab:inverse}
reports both boundary conditions.

That paper conjectured that gains beyond simple shapes ``are believed to be
minimal'' but had no way to test it. Free-form optimisation measures what
remains: $0.14\%$ on the cantilever, $1.45\%$ on the fixed--fixed beam.

\subsection{Comparison with the standard pipeline}
Table~\ref{tab:methods} places this work against how MEMS design with machine
learning is currently done. Zhang \emph{et al.} \cite{zhang} generate
$48{,}000$ finite-element simulations, fit an MLP, and inverse-design through
that surrogate, which returns a design in $0.01$\,s where a full FEA solve
takes $22$\,s. The contrast is the dataset column: we store no simulations at
all, because the gradient comes from the physics, and still emit a design in
$0.04$\,ms.

The lower rows re-implement both surrogate families on our own task. At an
equal budget of 400 true solves an MLP reaches $17.20$\,V and an FNO
$14.25$\,V, against $13.78$\,V for direct differentiation and $13.64$\,V for
the network. The FNO is competitive on quality; what separates us is needing no
training data, no architecture search and $40\times$ less wall-clock. One
forward pass lands inside the $0.28\%$ spread across optimiser restarts.

\subsection{Recovering travel lost to tolerance}
The tip-in ceiling $r_{\rm ti}=1/(1+k_xL_c^2/6k_\theta)$ varies device to
device and cannot be observed at test time, so fixed-gain control must aim at
the worst case and forfeits $19\%$ of travel. A policy seeing only the device's
own tilt response recovers $80.4\pm4.9\%$ of that range, $12.8\%$ more usable
travel, over $1{,}536$ held-out episodes with $0.0\pm0.0\%$ destroyed.

Reduced conservatism alone would give the same average, so the average is not
the evidence. Adaptation shows up in the correlation between the travel a
device achieves and its own ceiling: $\rho=+0.96$ against $-0.03$ for fixed
gain (Fig.~\ref{fig:ai}(a)). The policy tracks what it cannot measure.


\section{Conclusion}

Solving the extended fold system directly turns pull-in from an obstacle into a
differentiable object, and the exact gradient that follows removes the dataset
from AI-driven MEMS design: against the $48{,}000$ simulations of the standard
pipeline \cite{zhang} we store none, and still emit a geometry in $0.04$\,ms.
On the benchmark of \cite{haluzan} this cuts drive voltage
$22.86\rightarrow13.60$\,V from geometry alone, and in closed loop the same
physics recovers $12.8\%$ of the travel fabrication tolerance otherwise forces
a designer to surrender, with no device destroyed. Absolute voltages still
carry an offset traced to anchor compliance a 1D model cannot represent; adding
it is our next step.
